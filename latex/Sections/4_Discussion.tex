\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Every major decision in G2G traces to a measured property of the workload. Snarl-tree factorisation is what makes the problem practical: it decomposes the alignment into independent per-snarl subproblems, each solved optimally by LAPJV, though the snarl pairing itself remains a greedy heuristic. LAPJV replaces brute force because matching cost explodes with $k$ (Finding~1); centralised priority dispatch replaces static partitioning because per-snarl work is heavy-tailed (Finding~2), an argument the static-partition ablation confirms directly (Table \ref{tab:ablation}); and the cache-oblivious layout plus flat sequence storage address the memory wall (Finding~3). Two stages were originally quadratic: anchor detection and snarl decomposition. The minimiser index removes the first; the windowed sink search of \S\ref{sec:algo} makes the second linear for the common small snarls, though large-span SV snarls still fall back to the $O(|V|)$ search---the pipeline is near-linear on the evaluated workloads (Fig.~\ref{fig:refdiffscaling}) because such snarls are rare, but would regress on SV-rich inputs.

\subsection{Limitations}
The decomposer is DAG-only: although an optional vg-snarl backend can supply snarl boundaries, the allele enumeration and DP assume acyclicity, so tandem repeats (cycles) are out of scope and full cyclic support is future work. Snarl pairing is greedy rather than globally optimal: anchors mitigate this but a full tree-matching pairing is future work. G2G is single-machine; the $K>2$ multi-graph alignment problem and a distributed engine are natural extensions, as is GPU acceleration of the per-snarl matching, which is embarrassingly parallel across snarls.
